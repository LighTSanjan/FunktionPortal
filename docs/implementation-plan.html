What the portal is for

The treasurer's month is mostly transcription. The same four facts — which classes ran, who attended, what the instructor is owed, which fund it comes from — get typed into an invoice, an attendance sheet, and a register, by hand, on the 15th of every month. Fee collection is worse: the club has never kept a record of who actually paid, only a lump sum banked in once a term.

The portal's job is to make each of those facts get entered exactly once. Everything else is a rendering of them.

Two classes of artefact

The distinction drives nearly every technical decision:

ClassDocumentsFormatHow the portal treats it
A Transaction Register, Unofficial Funds Register Native Google Sheets Edited in place via the Sheets API, behind a diff-and-approve gate with a pre-write snapshot
B Invoices, attendance sheets, income deposit forms, PRF, PAF .docx / .xlsx Template filled server-side, handed to the browser as a download. Never written back to Drive

Class B needs no Google credentials at all, and the output stays byte-identical in layout to what OSL has accepted for years. Only the register requires API access, which keeps the trusted surface small.

Reference numbering

The labelling protocol defines four independent series, each resetting to 0001 at the start of the financial year. They are numbered separately from one another, and the hyphens are mandatory.

SeriesDocumentFY2026 form
IVInvoiceIV26-097-0008
SCFStudent Expense ClaimSCF26-097-0001
POPurchase OrderPO26-097-0001
INCIncome Deposit FormINC26-097-0001

Numbers are allocated by a single atomic UPDATE … RETURNING inside the same transaction that writes the document, against a unique constraint on (series, financialYear, sequence). Application-level max + 1 is the classic way to issue the same invoice number twice under concurrency, and it is exactly the kind of error that is invisible until OSL rejects a submission.

Issued numbers are never deleted. Voiding a document marks the number void with a reason, so a gap in the series has an answer.

Where FY2026 actually stands

The IV series is spent up to 0009. 0001 and 0002 went to the previous exco; the rest covered the June and July coach payments. August ran no classes, so nothing was raised against it.

The counter therefore seeds at 0010, not 0003. Seeding it lower would hand out numbers already on documents sitting with the Office of Finance — the one failure mode the atomic allocation below cannot save you from, because the collision happened before the portal existed.

The other three series have no FY2026 documents against them yet and start at 0001.

Discrepancies in the INC series

The FY2026 register records the summer collection as INC-26-097-0001, with a hyphen after the prefix. The protocol gives INC24-020-0001 as the pattern, so the correct form is INC26-097-0001. It was not a one-off: FY2025 carries the same hyphen on both of its term collections, so the malformed shape has already been copied forward once.

FY2025 also prints the Art Fiesta receipt's reference as INC25-097-0003 4 — with 0003 already spent on the Spring Term collection four rows above it. Read as 0004 on import, which is the only number that leaves the series intact, and flagged here because the sheet is still the authority.

Who issues the invoice

Design constraint

The instructor issues the invoice, not the club. Section 3.3 describes a coach appointed by SUTD under a Contract For Service and lists the submission as “Invoice issued by Coach/Instructor”; section 3.1.2 requires it to be billed to Singapore University of Technology and Design and calls the issuer the vendor. The club's part is to countersign — adding StuOrg name, treasurer name, position and signature, with the reference number in the top right corner — and forward it to OSL.

So a FUNKtion letterhead on the invoice would misstate who is billing the university, on a document that goes to the Office of Finance for payment. That is worth avoiding regardless of how much better it would look.

The layout itself is fair game, though, and the current one is doing the instructors no favours. The redesign keeps the instructor as issuer and improves everything else: a proper masthead carrying the instructor's own name and address, the reference number set clearly in the top right where OSL expects it, tabular figures in the line-item table, a real total row, and an unambiguous countersignature block.

Where FUNKtion branding genuinely belongs is on the documents the club actually authors — and there it should be used properly:

  • Coaches Monthly Attendance Sheet — the club's document, verified by the Treasurer
  • Purchase Request and Award Forms — raised by the club
  • Internal fee statements, term summaries, and the handover pack
Template correction

The sheet is verified by the Treasurer — the officer who signs the live submissions, and the only one holding the club fund balance the sheet quotes directly above the signature. The rebuilt version keeps that block and prints the signatory's name, office and student ID beneath the rule, rather than the template's bare “Name of Treasurer” label.

Guests

Two contracted coaches teach the standing weekdays. The club also books guest instructors for the occasional workshop, and they raise invoices like anyone else — at their own rate, for their own style, with no contract on file.

They are marked as guests rather than added to the roster, so they neither appear in the standing coach list nor get treated as though a contract exists to check their rate against. The portal also refuses to generate their invoice until it has a payee name and a bank account, because those details are typed on the day the invoice is due rather than checked months earlier when a contract was filed — and an invoice missing them looks complete and pays nobody. The attendance sheet is still produced either way: whether the paperwork is finished is a different question from whether the class happened.

The sheet itself is a member-by-date matrix of YES and NO, with the instructor as its first row. A date where that row reads NO is a self-practice session: the club met, the attendance counts, and nobody bills for it. The invoice is derived from that row rather than kept beside it, which is what stops the two documents disagreeing about how many sessions the coach actually taught.

The portal recomputes those totals rather than reading them, because a subtotal that has drifted from the rows above it is exactly the kind of error the sheet cannot show you.

Two things the sheet cannot tell you, and so an import stops for a human in the middle:

The date headers carry no year

13 Aug is unambiguous inside a trimester and ambiguous outside one. Resolved from the linked term's date range; a header that will not resolve fails the import rather than guessing a year. The weekday pattern doubles as a check on the result — a year that turns the whole term into Wednesdays is the wrong year.

The sheet never records which coach taught

Columns are dates, not classes. Invoices are per instructor per month, so every date column has to be assigned to an instructor before sessions can be written. In practice the pattern is fixed — Mondays are Jin, Thursdays are KS — so the portal seeds the assignment from the weekday and asks the treasurer to confirm.

The confirm step is not ceremony. Of the sixteen dates in the Tri 2 sheet, fifteen are a Monday or a Thursday and one, 26 May, is a Tuesday. A rule that is right fifteen times out of sixteen is exactly the kind that gets trusted on the sixteenth, and the cost of being wrong is an invoice billed against the wrong coach's contract.

Birthdays are stored in three pieces, not as a date

The birthday column is reliable in its day and month and not much else. The current sheet carries 19/6/2022 and 23/9/2022 — year typos for 2002 — and one 03/02/XXXX with no year at all. Day and month are correct in every one of those, so they are stored separately and the year is optional. A single date column would have to reject all three rows, and birthday messages only ever needed the day and the month.

The one column left behind

Medical and dietary notes stay in the sheet. Nothing in the portal reads them, and health data held for no purpose is a liability with no matching benefit. Everything else on the roster comes across.

Where each fact lives

Two systems will hold overlapping information about the same people, and the question of which one is right has to be answered per fact, not per system. Answering it per system is what produces the familiar outcome where both are wrong in different ways and nobody trusts either.

FactOwned byHow the other one sees it
Who is on the roster, contact details, birthday, status labelDrivePortal mirrors it, read-only
Who attended which classDrivePortal mirrors it, read-only
Fees: declarations, confirmations, tier, routePortalA per-term summary is filed to Drive when collection closes
Payment screenshotsPortalNever leaves the portal — see below
Reference numbers, invoices, attendance sheets, purchase formsPortalGenerated as files and filed back to Drive
Transaction RegisterPortal computes, Drive publishesThe only Drive artefact the portal writes to, and only through an approved diff

So Drive stays authoritative, and the instinct behind that is right: the secretary already records attendance there, at the moment it happens, and a portal that demanded they stop would simply be worked around. Data entered where the work happens is data that gets entered.

But it can only be authoritative about facts it actually holds. Columns J and K — Fees and Active — are empty in every row of every trimester sheet. There is no fee record in Drive to defer to. The portal is not overriding an authority there; it is filling a gap that has always been a gap.

Drive is authoritative per trimester, never continuously

Each trimester gets a fresh spreadsheet, and the roster is carried across by hand. There is no single roster in Drive — there are three snapshots that have already drifted. Student 1000789 appears in the Tri 2 active roster as one name and in the exit tab of the same file as a different name, with identical ID, email, phone and birthday. Whether that is a rename or a paste that missed a cell, matching on name would have created two people out of one.

This is the argument for the portal owning identity even while Drive owns the roster: continuity across trimesters is a fact no single sheet holds.

How they stay in step

  • Student ID is the join key, everywhere. Names are typed by hand and change; IDs do not.
  • Import is a diff you approve, not a sync that runs. New rows, changed fields and departures are shown before anything is written.
  • Import never deletes. A member who leaves is marked inactive. Their fee payments still reference them, and a treasurer looking at last term needs the row to still be there.
  • Portal-owned fields are not importable. The importer works from a whitelist of columns, so a stray edit to the sheet cannot reach a payment record.
  • A correction made here survives the next import. Fix a birth year in the portal and the field is marked as overridden; the next import stops offering to put the typo back. Otherwise the same diff is rejected every month until someone stops reading diffs, and that is the failure that actually costs you.

The monthly run

One input, five outputs. This is the flow that has to work first.

Fee collection

The control that matters most in the whole system is the gap between a member saying they paid and the exco confirming it. They are separate columns, written by different users, under different permissions. A member can never mark their own payment confirmed.

A declaration is not accepted without a screenshot of the PayNow confirmation. That screenshot is the entire evidence base for the confirmation that follows, so the portal treats a declaration without one as incomplete rather than pending.

Filing documents back to Drive

Everything the portal generates can also be filed into Drive, and should be: a document that only exists as a download in someone's browser is a document the next treasurer will not find. That includes a per-term fee summary — who paid, at which tier, how much, confirmed when — which is the one artefact the current process has no equivalent of.

A free Google account has 15 GB shared across Gmail, Drive and Photos. The generated documents come to roughly 18 MB a year — about a tenth of one percent. Ten years of club history would not fill a gigabyte. The existing trimester spreadsheets are 23–26 KB each, which is the scale everything here operates at.

The one category that is genuinely large is payment screenshots, and they are also the one category that should not go to Drive. They carry the payer's name and often a partial account number, and Drive permissions are granted per folder — there is no folder setting that means “the exco, and nobody else, ever”. They stay in the portal's private storage, where access is decided per row. Drive gets the summary; the portal keeps the evidence.

The real constraint is who owns the file, not how big it is

A Google service account has a storage quota of zero and cannot own files. It can happily read and modify a document that has been shared with it — which is exactly what the Transaction Register sync does — but the moment it tries to create one, Drive returns storageQuotaExceeded against an empty account. The usual escapes, shared drives and domain-wide delegation, both need Google Workspace, which a free club Gmail account is not.

So the portal uses two different keys

OperationAuthenticates asWhy
Read the trimester attendance sheetsService accountReading a shared file needs no quota. Unattended, no token to expire.
Update the Transaction RegisterService accountModifying an existing file, not creating one.
File a generated document into DriveThe signed-in treasurerOnly a real account can own a new file.

Filing is a button someone presses, not a scheduled job, so it runs inside a live session and never needs an offline refresh token. That sidesteps a trap worth knowing about: an OAuth app left in Testing publishing status has its refresh tokens expire after seven days, which would break any unattended Drive write about a week after it started working, in a way that looks like the code broke rather than the consent screen.

The scope to ask for is drive.file — per-file access to documents the app created or the user picked. It is non-sensitive, so it needs no Google verification review, and it cannot read anything in Drive the treasurer has not handed over. The broad drive and drive.readonly scopes are restricted, require a security assessment to publish, and would grant the portal the club's entire Drive to do a job that needs two folders.

Procurement

The form type and approval chain are derived from the amount. Neither is a choice the requester makes.

Access and controls

Two roles. Exco sees and does everything; members see their own record and nothing else.

Offices are for routing, not for access

Alongside the role, an exco member holds an office — president, vice-president, treasurer, assistant treasurer, secretary. It confers nothing. Every exco member can already reach every part of the portal, and the office changes only who gets told about what, and whose title prints under a signature.

Most of the routing below lands on the treasurer, which is the honest picture: they raise the claim, they verify the sheet, they hold the fund balance, and they own the register diff. The other offices matter for the approvals above $300, where a second authority is the point.

This is worth keeping apart. The moment an office grants access, adding one becomes a security decision and removing one becomes a lockout; kept as a label, a mid-year reshuffle is a dropdown. It also fixes the notification problem directly: a queued register diff that pings the whole exco is a diff nobody reviews.

How somebody gets access

Two directions, and the club needs both. An invitation is an exco member naming an email and the person accepting it when they sign in. A request is the reverse: somebody signs in, finds themselves on nobody's roster, and asks to be let in. Invitation is the primary path because it is the one where the club decides first; requests are the fallback that stops a treasurer having to be reachable before anyone can be useful.

Rule

A request is never self-approvable. The approver has to be a different active exco member, and both halves — who asked, who granted — are written to the audit log. A request that could be approved by the person who raised it is not an approval, it is a formality.

Joining and being promoted are different requests

Anyone with a Google account can reach the sign-in page, so the two have to be scored differently. Asking to join as a member is low-stakes: the roster is imported from Drive anyway, and the worst case is a stranger who sees their own empty fee record. Asking for exco is a privilege-escalation request — it reaches the roster, every payment, the fund balances and the register.

So they are separate request types with separate queues, and the exco one names what it grants in the approval screen rather than saying “approve”. An approver who has not been told they are handing over the payment records is an approver who clicks yes.

The office is not the request

“Request to become treasurer” is really two things, and keeping them apart is what preserves the property in the section above. The role — exco — is the access. The office — treasurer — is a label that routes notifications and prints under a signature, and confers nothing.

So the request grants the role, and the office is assigned separately by an exco member afterwards. Collapsing them would make adding an office a security decision, which is exactly what the offices-are-labels design exists to avoid: at that point a mid-year reshuffle stops being a dropdown and becomes a lockout risk.

Constraints worth building in from the start

  • One open request per person at a time. The sign-in page is reachable by anyone; without this the queue is a spam surface, and a queue nobody wants to read is a queue where things get approved unread.
  • Requests expire. An approval acting on a six-month-old request is approving a decision nobody is still thinking about.
  • Revocation is the same screen. The exco list and the request queue belong together, because the handover month is the one time both happen at once — and an outgoing exco who is never removed is the most common way a club's access list rots.
  • The first exco cannot come from here. On an empty database there is no one to approve anything, so the environment-allowlist bootstrap stays. It disarms itself once any active membership exists.

There is a second reason to build this rather than adding exco by hand in the database: the approval is the handover record. “Who let this person into the club's finances, and when” is a question the audit log should be able to answer in March, and a row inserted straight into Postgres answers it with silence.

Stored signatures

Signing the same three documents by hand every month is the kind of friction that ends with a stack of pre-signed blanks. Instead each signatory uploads their signature once, on their own profile page, and the portal stamps it while composing the document. Built; see Reading, signing and sending below for how it behaves in the monthly run.

  • The stamp follows the authorisation, never replaces it. A signature is applied at the moment its owner presses the button that authorises the document, in the same request. It is never applied on anyone else's behalf, and never on a schedule.
  • The instructor's signature has to come from the instructor. The invoice is their document billed to the university. The club stamping a coach's signature onto a claim the club prepared would forge the one attestation OSL is relying on, so the specimen is supplied by the instructor and used only on their own invoices.
  • Specimens are forgeable material and are stored as such. Private bucket, no public path, read server-side only, short-lived signed URLs where a preview is genuinely needed. Uploading or replacing one is an audited event.

Controls

  • Authorisation is server-side on every mutation. A role claimed by the client is never an input to a decision.
  • Amounts are computed, not accepted. Claim totals come from session count multiplied by the contracted rate; fee amounts come from the term's configured tier. The browser never posts a dollar figure.
  • Row-level security on Supabase as defence in depth, so a leaked anon key does not expose the member roster.
  • Every financial write is audited — actor, before, after — using the audit log inherited from the kernel.
  • The register write is snapshotted before it runs and shown as a diff before it is authorised.
  • Secrets stay server-side. The Google service account key and Telegram bot token are never in the client bundle.
On the word hardened

What is achievable in three weeks is good defaults applied consistently: server-side authorisation, RLS, audited writes, validated input, no client-trusted money. That is not the same as a security review. Budget a dedicated pass well before handover, while there is still time to act on what it finds.

Invoices exist; full contract management does not

The monthly coach-payment workflow is already end to end: attendance determines billable sessions, the portal applies the effective rate, allocates the reference, freezes a snapshot, produces the invoice and attendance pack, records the club’s signature and tracks sent and paid states. Instructor profiles also store a contract reference and a rate history. What remains for phase 05 is administration of the contract itself — its document and approvals, total hour allowance, usage and remaining hours, expiry and renewal, evaluation and Purchase Award Form. Those are related to invoicing, but they are not required to generate a correct monthly invoice from an already-approved rate.

On account ownership

Supabase moves projects between organisations with the database intact, so no dump and restore is needed. The risk is not the hosting, it is the data: once real members have signed in and real payments are recorded, a migration that goes wrong is a genuine problem. Demonstrating to the exco with seeded data, and holding off real records until the club accounts exist, reduces the migration to code and schema alone.

Design language

The logo is built from one idea — the same shape stacked four times in vermilion, marigold, sky and indigo, each offset down and right, like the layers of a kueh lapis. That is a system, not just a mark, and the interface runs on it.

The stack

  • Buttons sit on a hard offset shadow in the next layer colour — no blur, no gradient. Pressing collapses the offset to zero and translates the button into its own shadow by exactly the offset distance, so the front face lands where its shadow was and the control physically compresses. The primary button carries the full three-layer stack; secondary and destructive carry one; ghost carries none, because it is a link wearing a button’s clothes and should not read as a physical object.
  • Fields hold the stack at zero offset when idle and fan it out to 2/4/6/8px on focus, lifting one pixel up and left so the face stays put and the layers appear to slide out from under it. Idle-to-focus is a transition on an existing shadow rather than a new one, which is the difference between the layers sliding and them blinking on.
  • Cards and panels carry a thin stacked edge on the bottom-right, two or three layers deep. Depth encodes importance: a flat card is routine, a three-layer card is the thing needing attention. Cards that are links lift towards the reader on hover and settle back on press.
  • Layer order is fixed — vermilion, marigold, sky, indigo, front to back — and the stack only ever falls down and to the right. Keeping both constant is what makes the device read as the logo rather than as decoration.

Where it lives in the code

All of it is a handful of classes in globals.css — lapis-field, lapis-press with lapis-stack or lapis-tap, lapis-plate, lapis-lift, lapis-band — over four brand tokens and a small motion scale (--ease-soft, --ease-spring, --dur-1…--dur-4) in tokens.css. Two curves and four durations for the whole portal, because what actually reads as smooth is everything accelerating the same way, not anything being slow. Entrances are one keyframe (rise-in) staggered by an inline --delay, so a server-rendered page gets its movement from CSS alone and nothing has to hydrate before the page can be looked at.

The brand inks are sampled from the logo artwork rather than eyeballed, so the band across the auth page and the layers behind a focused field are the same four inks the wordmark is drawn in.

Light by default

The portal now defaults to light rather than following the operating system. The palette it is drawn in — warm paper, four bright inks, a photograph — is a light one, and served to a laptop set to dark it came out as a black page with an orange button on it. Dark is still reachable from the profile menu; it is just no longer inherited. The class is set by a synchronous inline script in the root layout, because React cannot put it on <html> before the browser paints.

The mark

The wordmark is a real vector now, traced from the club’s own artwork by a small contour tracer (crack-boundary walk, Douglas–Peucker, then re-smoothed to quadratics; the script is in the scratch notes, not the repo). It replaces a PNG that was exported at one size and went soft the moment anything asked it to be bigger. A separate funktion-mark.svg carries a redrawn layered F for the places too small for the wordmark — a crop of the real logo was tried first and turns to mush below about 32px, because the drawn artwork carries hairline highlights that disappear before the letter does.

Where it stops

Registers, figures and tables stay quiet. A finance screen has to be read quickly and trusted, and stacking every row would fight that. The layered treatment belongs to controls, navigation and status — the things you act on — while the numbers themselves sit on plain ground in tabular figures.

Two constraints keep it usable: offset alone never carries meaning that colour or text does not also carry, since depth is invisible to a screen reader; and under prefers-reduced-motion the movement is removed while the states themselves are not — a focused field still shows its stack, it just arrives instantly. Reduced motion is a request for less movement, not for less feedback.

The club’s front page

The dashboard answers four questions in order: what needs a person today, what money is left, how big the club is right now, and who teaches it.

  • Alerts lead. The deadlines in the guidelines are the kind that are invisible until they are missed — an invoice due three working days after its own date, a seed fund that stops accepting submissions in February — so the page opens with them rather than with balances. Each row is one sentence about one thing and links to the screen that clears it. The empty state is stated rather than implied: “nothing is on fire” is information a treasurer wants confirmed.
  • Funds are shown as quantities. What is left is set at display size; the opening balance and what has been drawn against it are the supporting figures, and a bar repeats the same three numbers in a form you can read without reading. Committed means invoices that are not void, plus purchases at approved or beyond — a request still working through treasurer and president is not yet a commitment.
  • Coaches are cards, not rows. The club knows these people; a table of names with warning badges beside them reads like a supplier ledger. Until there are photographs, the plate is the coach’s initials over one of the four inks, keyed to their name so a given coach is always the same colour.
  • Money is exco-only — not because the numbers are secret, but because a member opening the portal to pay their subscription should not have to scroll past a fund report to find it. A member sees the greeting, the club’s size, who teaches, and how to ask for wider access.

What is built

As of this pass the signed-in portal is complete end to end for the coach claim:

  • Classes (/:org/sessions) — a month at a time, because a month is the unit everything downstream works in. Record a class, correct one, mark the register. The whole roster is written on every save, present and absent alike: an absent member with no row is indistinguishable from a member nobody got round to marking.
  • Attendance import (/:org/attendance) — upload a trimester CSV export, review member and instructor matching, then apply the sessions and marks. The spreadsheet is parsed and audited before any register rows are written.
  • Monthly run (/:org/monthly-run) — a status board rather than a wizard, because the work is several coaches at different stages. Raising a month allocates the IV reference inside the same transaction that writes the invoice, checks the coach is invoiceable before allocating, and produces both documents. Defaults to last month, since a claim covers a finished one.
  • Documents are rebuilt from the stored rows on download rather than kept as blobs, so there is one copy of the truth and a document downloaded twice cannot differ from itself. The two values fixed at generation — the reference number and the invoice date — are read back rather than recomputed.
  • Profile (/:org/settings/profile) — the page the Settings link lands on. Editing your own name matters because it prints under the treasurer's signature, and Google hands over whatever the account is called.
  • Password reset — /forgot-password and /reset-password. The recovery branch of /auth/confirm had redirected to a page that did not exist.
Two rules the database now enforces about a raised month

A class that is on an invoice cannot be deleted or edited, checked two ways: the invoice line records which class it bills, and, separately, the coach's month is checked for any non-void invoice. Either check alone has a hole — a line whose link was never written passes the first, and the second is what catches it. Changing a raised month is worse than deleting from it, because a deletion leaves an obvious gap while a changed duration silently makes a submitted invoice stop following from the register.

The transaction registers

Both of the club's registers are imported and readable at /:org/register: the OSL one the university reads — three financial years of it, FY2024 through FY2026 — and the unofficial one the exco keep themselves, which runs from 2017 and is the only thing that makes the unofficial balance explicable rather than merely asserted. 230 rows, filterable by fund and by year.

FY2024 is as far back as the OSL side goes. Earlier registers survive only as the figures carried forward into FY2024's opening rows; the sheets themselves did not come through a handover.

The spreadsheets remain the authority — OSL open the sheet, not this — so the screen is read-only. Rows enter through the seeded import in prisma/data; new spend enters as invoices and purchase requests, which is what the register sync in phase 07 pushes back the other way.

Why the import is trustworthy

It is a hand transcription of somebody else's bookkeeping — nine years of it, in a sheet with merged cells, three separate blocks all labelled “Subtotal for 2021”, and dates in four formats. What makes it safe to rely on is that both sheets publish their own control totals, and every one of them is asserted by the tests in prisma/data/__tests__: each section's totals row, each year's subtotal, and the summary box's total credit, total debit and net balance. Correcting a figure without correcting its total fails the suite.

Each OSL year is checked twice over: every section's totals row, and then the “Total Balance” figure printed in the sheet header. The second check is the one that earns its keep — a row filed under the wrong section reconciles both totals it touches and still moves the year's balance. FY2024's rows land on $1,842.79 and FY2025's on $2,620.47, which is what those two headers say.

The two derivations then agree independently: the FY2026 ledger rows net to $4,470.35, and the three fund accounts — built from separate figures — leave $4,470.35.

Three cents, twice

FY2024 closes its income fund at $1,842.79 and FY2025 opens at $1,842.82. FY2025 closes at $2,620.47 and FY2026 opens at $2,620.44. Three cents appear at one year boundary and disappear at the next.

Not a defect to chase. These carried-forward figures are set by OSL and confirmed against their own records; the club cannot restate them, and the two drifts cancel. Pinned by a test so the numbers stay as the sheets state them, and recorded here so the next treasurer who reconciles one year against the next finds the answer rather than the question.

Two further things the OSL sheets record only by accident. IV24-097-0024 appears nowhere — the FY2024 series jumps 0023 to 0025 — and a gap in a running number is exactly what OSL queries. And in both FY2024 and FY2025 the July locking fee is one invoice split across two funds, because the seed fund ran out mid-invoice; one reference number, two register rows, which is why the import keys on position rather than on the number.

One thing the sheets cannot supply is dates. Forty of the unofficial rows carry only the year they were filed under, and a few carry a term (“Throughout Term 3”) instead. Those are stored with a null date and shown as their financial year; inventing a date to fill the column would make the table tidier and the record worse.

Attendance import

The club records attendance in a Google Sheet per trimester and has since 2017: the roster down the left, one column per class date across the top, a 1 where somebody turned up, and a subtotal row under each block of members. The portal reads that sheet rather than replacing it, at /:org/attendance.

It takes a CSV export, not a Drive connection. The treasurer downloads the attendance tab and uploads it. That is a deliberate trade: a service account with standing read access to the club's Drive is a larger thing to hold than a file somebody chose to hand over, and the register sync in phase 07 is the only place that access is actually unavoidable.

The sheet knows who taught

This schema was written expecting a human to assign every date to a coach by hand, on the reasoning that a sheet recording who attended cannot record who taught. That turns out to be wrong for these sheets. Two INSTRUCTOR rows sit below the roster with a mark against each date they took, and a legend above them reads 1 -> 1.5h; 2 -> 3h.

So the mapping is read from the sheet and the treasurer confirms it, rather than typing twenty-six assignments from scratch. It reconciles: the marks give Jin 16.5 hours and KS 19.5 in Trimester 1, and Jin 10.5 and KS 12 in Trimester 2, which is exactly what the Total Hours block the treasurer keeps for the contract renewal says. The legend is read from the cell rather than hard-coded, so a term that changes the class length reconciles on its own instead of quietly billing the old one.

The date headers carry no year — just “18 May” — which is why an import is filed against a term and why the term's own start and end dates resolve them. A header that lands outside the selected term is an error rather than a guess, because a class placed in the wrong year lands on the wrong month's coach invoice.

What the sheets turned out to contain

  • Subtotals that disagree with the marks above them. Three cells in Trimester 1, four in Trimester 2. The marks are imported — they are what people actually ticked — and the disagreements are listed on the review screen rather than resolved silently.
  • Classes nobody is recorded as teaching. Four dates in Trimester 1 and one in Trimester 2, including 26 May, when twenty-five people attended and neither coach is marked. Self-practice, almost certainly. A ClassSession requires an instructor, so these stay out of the import unless somebody assigns one — which is the honest outcome, not a silent drop.
  • Birthdays in two different formats. The column header declares DDMMYY and most rows follow it, but five are plainly month-first (“9/18/2003”). Those are read the other way round and reported; anything still unreadable is left blank rather than guessed, because the club uses this to send birthday messages and a transposed date sends it on the wrong day.
  • A roster row with no student ID. It is set aside rather than created: keyed on nothing, it would be created fresh by every future import.

Matching is on student ID first, then email, then an exact name — and never on a name two people share, which is a real case here. Everything unmatched is shown before anything is written.

The import reconciles against the register

Trimesters 1 and 2 of 2026 are loaded: 54 members, 37 classes, 802 attendance marks. The check that makes it trustworthy is that those classes reproduce the money.

Every one of the ten FY2026 coach payments in the OSL register comes back out of the attendance sheets exactly, at the contract rate of $90 an hour — March through July, both coaches, from $135.00 to $405.00. The register was transcribed by hand from a spreadsheet; the attendance came out of a different spreadsheet through a parser. Nothing connects them but the classes actually having happened.

It also rescues the four register rows that carry no reference number. Their invoice dates are useless — all four are dated 17 August, covering June and July — but the attendance sheets confirm what each is for: 3.0h and 4.5h of June, 4.5h and 1.5h of July. The numbers were never allocated, but the work behind them is now evidenced.

Reading, signing and sending

The month’s two documents can now be read on screen at /:org/monthly-run/:invoice, signed there, and sent from there. It is three steps in that order, on one page, because they are three steps in that order.

The preview is the document

Showing somebody a document before they sign it is only worth anything if the thing they looked at is the thing that gets sent. Two renderers over one data model — a .docx builder and a web page — can drift apart silently, and the failure is invisible precisely because the preview still looks right.

So both are reduced to their text and compared, character for character, by a test. Layout may differ; content may not. Add a line to one renderer and the suite fails until it is in the other. The comparison strips the .docx core properties first, because a file’s title and build timestamps are the wrapper, not the document.

One PDF, from the browser

§3.1.2 wants the pack as a single PDF. The club has no document converter and no licence for one, but every browser can print to PDF — so /:org/monthly-run/:invoice/print is the same two previews laid out to print, invoice first, attendance sheet second, one per page. The portal’s chrome is marked data-print="hide" rather than matched on class names, since “this is chrome, not document” is a fact about the element while a class name is a styling decision that will change.

Signing is one act, not two

§3.3 has the Treasurer verify the attendance sheet; §3.1.2 has the club countersign the coach’s invoice. That is one person making one statement, so it is one button. Signing them separately would let the two documents disagree about who stood behind the month.

  • The office is captured at signing, not read at render. A treasurer who hands over in March signed as treasurer in February, and the document has to keep saying so.
  • The specimen used is recorded on the sheet. Documents are rebuilt from their rows on every download; without this, replacing a signature would silently restamp every document already sent to the Office of Finance. For the same reason a specimen that has been stamped on something survives its owner replacing it — one that was never stamped is just a file, and goes.
  • Only the club’s half is ever stamped. The coach signs their own invoice. There is no code path that puts a coach’s signature on a claim the club prepared.
  • Signing without a specimen is allowed. It records who verified and prints an empty rule to sign by hand, which is what the club did before this existed. Refusing would make an uploaded image a precondition for doing the paperwork at all.
  • A signature can be withdrawn until it is sent. After that, correcting a month means voiding it: the signed document exists outside the portal by then, and un-signing the copy here would only make the two disagree.

Sending is still a person writing an email

The portal composes the message — recipient, subject carrying the reference, a body quoting the invoice number, date, amount and fund — and opens the treasurer’s mail client. It sends nothing itself. The pack goes from the club’s own address, which is the address the Office of Finance already replies to, and a portal that sent on the treasurer’s behalf would be one more place a submission could go missing with no sent-items folder to check.

Recording a month as sent now requires it to have been signed. Marking something sent that was never verified puts the portal’s own record out of step with the document OSL received, and the run card no longer offers a route to it that skips reading the documents.

Storage policies are still a dashboard job

The private bucket did not exist on the hosted project and now does. Row-level security on storage.objects is on and the bucket carries no policies, so the signed-in user’s own token can reach nothing in it — and the migration role cannot add policies, because that table belongs to supabase_storage_admin.

So signature reads and writes go through the service role, with authorisation done in the action and object keys derived from the session rather than accepted from the caller. That is sound, but it is one layer where there should be two. The policies want adding from the Supabase SQL editor as a hardening pass.

What a signed month is made of

A second pass over the signing work — a review by a separate model, run against the diff — found that the two documents were still being rebuilt on every download from live class, rate, roster and instructor rows, while the invoice row kept the total it was issued with. The guards stop a class in a raised month being edited. Nothing stopped a rate being backdated, a member joining the roster, a coach changing bank account, or attendance being re-marked, and each of those silently changes a document already sitting with the Office of Finance.

A raised month is frozen

Raising a month now captures it: the coach’s details, the roster, and every class with who was in it, stored as a snapshot beside the invoice. Every later rendering — preview, print sheet, both .docx downloads — reads that snapshot and the invoice’s own lines, and touches no live row at all. Each line carries the date it bills as a field rather than as a substring of its description.

A month raised before this existed cannot be reproduced as it was issued, so the portal says so and refuses rather than rendering today’s rows in its place. There are none; the check is there so there never are.

What else that pass turned up

  • The signatory’s name was not frozen either. Only the user id was stored, and rendering dereferenced that account’s current name — which its owner can edit, and has to be able to, because Google hands over whatever the account is called. One rename would have re-attributed every document already submitted. The name is now captured at signing.
  • The office was invented. An exco member with no named post was recorded as TREASURER. That is the portal making a claim nobody made, on the one line the Office of Finance reads to know who stood behind the month. It now records no office and prints “Exco member”, and the signing screen says so before you press the button rather than after.
  • Two people could sign the same month. Both would read it as unsigned, both would write, and the second would quietly replace the first’s name, post, specimen and timestamp while both were told it worked. Signing is now conditional on the sheet still being unsigned and checks that it claimed exactly one row.
  • A raised month was still reachable three other ways. Creating a class in one was unchecked; editing one checked only the month it was leaving, not the one it was joining; marking attendance was unchecked; and the attendance import rewrote classes and marks for any month at all. All four are closed, and the import now reports the dates it left alone rather than skipping them silently.
  • Deleting an unreferenced specimen was wrong twice over. The reference count raced a concurrent signing, and it ran under row-level security, so references in an organisation the user had since left were invisible to it. Specimens are no longer deleted at all: replacing one stops the account pointing at it, and the object stays for the documents that were stamped with it.
  • A fake PNG passed validation. Eight magic bytes and two plausible integers are easy to produce by accident — a truncated download manages it — and the file would validate on upload and fail months later while composing a document somebody was waiting to sign. The image header is now checked for real, and a zero dimension is refused at the point of reading it.
Signing without a stamp stays allowed

Verifying with no specimen on file records who stood behind the month and prints an empty rule to sign by hand, which is what the club did before any of this existed. Refusing would make uploading an image a precondition for doing the paperwork at all.

What it must not do is go out looking finished. The send step now says plainly that the printout has to be signed by hand before it is attached.

Self-practice

A class session no longer needs a coach. The column was NOT NULL, which meant the portal could only record the weeks somebody was paid for, and the 2026 import put a number on what that cost: five dates across two trimesters could not be recorded at all — 26 May among them, with twenty-five people present. A week with no coach is not a broken week. It is a normal week in a dance club.

Those five dates are now in the database, with the 133 attendance marks that were being discarded with them. They were recovered without the original spreadsheets: every import keeps the sheet it read, so scripts/db/reapply-import.ts re-runs a stored parse under the new rules. Coached hours are unchanged by it — Trimester 1 still gives Jin 16.5 and KS 19.5, Trimester 2 Jin 10.5 and KS 12, which is what the treasurer’s own Total Hours block says.

Three answers about a date, not two

“Nobody taught it” and “we cannot work out who taught it” used to collapse into the same blank, and that is exactly how five real dates ended up recorded nowhere. They are now separate outcomes:

  • A coach — the sheet names somebody the portal knows. Billable.
  • Self-practice — people attended and no coach is marked. The sheet is not being vague; it is saying the club met on its own. Written as a class with no instructor.
  • Left out — nobody attended, or the sheet names a coach the portal does not have. A question for a human, and it stays a question rather than quietly becoming self-practice.

What it does to the documents

A self-practice date appears on every coach’s attendance sheet for that month with the instructor row reading NO, and on no invoice. That is what the club’s own trimester sheets already do, and it is the line that explains to OSL why the grid shows more dates than the invoice bills.

It follows that a self-practice date is locked by any coach’s raised month, not just its own — it is printed on a sheet that has already been submitted, and the sheet is the evidence the invoice rests on. Moving it would change a document the Office of Finance is holding.

Two smaller consequences worth writing down. Postgres counts NULLs as distinct, so the unique key on (org, coach, date) stopped constraining anything the moment the coach became optional; a partial unique index covers self-practice separately. And the map that links an invoice line back to the class it bills is built from coach-led sessions only, since a self-practice date sharing a day with a real class would otherwise overwrite the entry and point the line at the wrong session.

Fee collection, built

Phase 02. The flow runs end to end at /:org/fees for a member and /:org/fees/collection for the exco: rates and dates per term, a member’s own amount due with the PayNow QR, a declaration with its screenshot, a reconciliation queue, and closing the collection into an Income Deposit Form.

Declaring and confirming are different acts by different people

They are separate permissions. fee.declare belongs to every member and can only ever write DECLARED; fee.manage is exco-only and is the only way to reach CONFIRMED. The confirming action refuses a payment the same person declared, which is the same reasoning as an access request nobody else approves: a decision somebody can make for themselves is not a control, it is a formality.

A declaration without a screenshot is refused rather than queued. That screenshot is the entire evidence base for the confirmation that follows, so a declaration without one is incomplete, not pending.

The browser never posts a figure

The amount comes from the term’s configured tier, and the tier is read off the roster label the club already keeps. Those labels are free text typed into a spreadsheet by four years of secretaries — the live roster carries “Confirmed”, “1/2 term”, “Confirmed; Exchange”, “May come back?” and one that reads “Confirmed; praying for swift recovery” — so the reader is generous with them and says how sure it is.

Three of the 46 chargeable rows come back uncertain, all of them the hedged ones, and they are listed on the collection screen before a figure is charged to anybody. A question mark in a status column is not somebody agreeing to pay seventy dollars.

The eight graduated rows are charged nothing at all rather than appearing on the board as debts nobody will collect. On the roster as it stands that is 42 full, 3 half, 1 exchange — $3,080.00, which derives to $2,825.69 net and $254.31 of GST.

GST is derived, and adds back up

The gross is what members actually transferred, so the split divides rather than multiplies: net = gross × 10000 / (10000 + rate), and the tax is the remainder rather than a second rounding. That is what guarantees net + GST = gross for every input, including the ones where rounding each part honestly and separately would come to a cent out. The register reconciles against a bank statement, not against arithmetic.

The rate is a settings row in basis points, not a constant. A GST change is a form.

What the portal does not do

It does not fill OSL’s Income Deposit Form. There has never been a copy of that template here, and authoring one from scratch would produce a document OSL may simply reject — the opposite of the rule that Class B output stays byte-identical to what has been accepted for years. Closing a collection instead derives every figure the form asks for — reference, WBS revenue element, gross, net, GST, the one-working-day deadline — and leaves the typing to a person.

What it does author is the club’s own record: a per-term summary of who paid, at which tier, how much, and confirmed when. That is the one artefact the current process has no equivalent of. Fees have been banked as a lump sum once a term with no record of who they came from, so “did I pay last term” has only ever been answerable by whoever was treasurer at the time, and only until they graduated.

Screenshots never leave the portal

A PayNow confirmation carries the payer’s name and often part of an account number. Drive permissions are granted per folder and there is no folder setting that means “the exco, and nobody else, ever”, so these stay in private storage and are opened through a redirect to a URL minted per request, good for two minutes, that never appears in a page anybody could save or forward. Drive gets the summary; the portal keeps the evidence.

Being recognised as yourself

A member opening the portal to pay has to be matched to their roster row. Signing in does it by email, which is the only key both sides hold — and only where exactly one unlinked row carries that address, so a shared or duplicated one links nobody.

That covers most people and will not cover somebody who signed up with a personal address. For them the exco links the account to the row by hand, on the collection screen, audited. Asking the member to pick their own row would be asking them to assert an identity, which is a different thing from confirming one.

What the second review found in the fee work

The same separate-model pass, run over fee collection. It reported the arithmetic and the reference allocation clean, and found seven things that were not. All are fixed.

  • A screenshot could be lifted out of the address bar. The exco-only route redirected to a two-minute signed URL, and a signed URL is a bearer token: it carries no identity, so copying it into another browser showed the screenshot until it expired. The bytes are streamed through the route instead, which puts the check on every request that fetches them rather than only on the one that minted a link.
  • A slow upload could undo a confirmation. Declaring checked the status, uploaded the screenshot outside the transaction, then wrote unconditionally. An exco member confirming during that window had their confirmation rolled back into a declaration. The write now re-checks what the read established and is conditional on the payment still being undecided.
  • A confirmed payer who then graduated vanished from the board while their money was still banked. The board started from the active roster; closing the collection aggregated every confirmed payment. So the screen previewed a smaller figure than the one that would go to OSL. Anybody with a payment against the term stays on the board, marked as having left, and owes nothing further.
  • A departed member was charged the full rate. tierFor returns null for “Graduated”, and null means owes nothing — but two paths read it as ?? 'FULL', which would have shown a graduated exco member a seventy-dollar bill and taken their money. Null is now handled as what it means, and the rule is pinned by a test.
  • An uncertain tier went quiet the moment somebody declared. The member is never asked which tier they are on, so their declaration carries the same guess — and the flag was cleared on sight of one, hiding the warning at exactly the moment somebody was about to confirm the guessed amount. It stays until the roster label is settled.
  • Both linking paths could cross organisations. Sign-in searched every roster in the database, including in the case where the auto-join had declined to pick an organisation because it could not tell which. The manual link scoped the roster row and not the account. Both are scoped now.
  • Two treasurers closing at once got a stack trace. The unique key on the deposit is what actually serialises them; the loser now gets a sentence.
The control is now in the database, not only in the code

The row-level policy on fee_payments grants a member full access to their own row, and its own comment conceded that the confirmed transition was “guarded in the application”. That is true of the portal and not of the database: the policies are granted to authenticated, which is the role PostgREST runs as, so anybody holding the publishable key and their own session could have updated their own row to CONFIRMED without going near the application. A control one HTTP request bypasses is not a control.

It is a trigger now. Moving into CONFIRMED requires an active exco member of the owning organisation who is not the person who declared it; a payment cannot be inserted already confirmed; rejection is held to the same rule. Service-role writes — the seed, the import scripts — pass, because they do not run as a person. Proved against the database rather than asserted: a member self-confirming, a member marking their own row rejected, a member inserting a confirmed row and an exco member confirming their own declaration are all refused, and an exco member confirming somebody else’s is allowed.

And one bug that was already there

Writing that trigger surfaced a latent fault in app_user_id(), the function every row-level policy depends on. Its comment says it “returns NULL rather than raising when unset, so a policy on an unauthenticated connection denies rather than errors”. That holds only while the setting has never been touched. dbAs installs the claims transaction-locally, so on a pooled connection the value reverts to the empty string once the transaction commits — not to unset — and the next caller evaluated ''::jsonb, which raises.

Nothing had hit it because every policy-relevant read happens inside dbAs. A trigger, a background job or an admin write on the same pooled connection would have. It was found by a probe against the real database rather than by reading the code: the function looks null-safe, and the difference between “unset” and “set to empty” is invisible at a glance.

Nothing here is about one club

The documents already read their branding, billing address and project code from organisation settings rather than from constants. Three things had not caught up.

  • Those settings had no screen. They do now, at /:org/settings/organisation: name, tagline, institution, submission address, project code, cluster, GST rate, the billing address, and the bands of the rule under the masthead. A rebrand, a change of project code, a campus move or a GST change is a form rather than a deployment.
  • The dashboard’s financial year was the literal 2026. It would have gone on quietly showing FY2026’s balances to the exco that took over in April 2027 — the one moment somebody most needs to see that the new year has nothing in it yet. It is derived from today, and where the new year has no fund accounts the most recent year that does is shown instead, with the hero saying which. The project code printed beside the reference counters was the literal 097; it comes from settings.
  • The sign-in page had no organisation in scope, so its name, tagline and wordmark could not come from the database. They come from one module with environment overrides instead, which is the right lifetime for them: the wordmark on a sign-in page is not something anybody should be able to change at runtime.

A test walks the whole document module — subdirectories included, since the preview renderers live one down — and fails if the word FUNKtion or a real coach’s name appears anywhere in it.

Organised by job, not by table

The sidebar was a flat list of seven — Your access, Register, Instructors, Classes, Attendance import, Subscription, Monthly run. That is the schema. A treasurer arrives thinking “the coaches need paying for August”, which spans three of those entries with nothing to say they belong together and no hint that two of them are inputs to the third.

A second opinion on the whole portal, asked for bluntly, came back with the same verdict and a list of concrete dead ends. It is now Home, then Work (Coach payments, Membership fees), then Records (Classes, Transactions, Coaches), then You. Grouping appears only once there is enough to group, so an ordinary member gets a flat list of three rather than three headings over three items.

  • Attendance import moved into settings. It is done once or twice a trimester and it is one of two ways into the same rows. It sat level with the month’s work, next to a screen that does the same job by hand, and the Classes screen claimed to be “the only place a date or a duration is entered” — which the import contradicts.
  • “Register” became “Transactions”. In a dance club, a register is the attendance register. The portal used the word for both, in the same sidebar.
  • The exco’s subscription entry goes to the collection, not to the member-facing page with the workspace hidden behind a quiet button on it.

Both jobs are modelled

A workflow says which steps are done, which one is live, when it is due, and the single next action — built on the server from the same rows the screens read, so a step cannot claim to be done when it is not. It appears as a card on the home screen and as a strip along the top of the screen that advances it, because a stepper that only exists on the home screen is one you have already left behind by the time you need it.

It names one next action rather than exposing every possible one. Where nothing the treasurer does would help — a month sitting with OSL, forty-five members who have not yet declared — it says so and offers no button, because inventing work and then nagging about it is worse than silence.

What building it turned up

The home screen could say everything was clear on the 14th

“Nothing needs you right now. Everything with a deadline on it is clear” appeared whenever there were no alerts — while the alert logic only ever looked at invoices that had already been raised. A month whose classes were recorded and whose documents had never been raised produced no alert at all. The empty state is gone; the two workflow cards carry that state now, and they are built from the classes as well as the invoices.

  • Terms could only be created by the seed script. Subscription collection called notFound() without one and the attendance import refused to load, so a club that had not been seeded — which is every club but this one — could never reach either. There is a create-term screen now, pre-filled with the trimester it probably is.
  • PAID and paidAt had existed since the schema was written with nothing that ever set them. Every submitted month sat at “with OSL” for good, and the board could not tell a month that was finished from one that had been ignored. Recordable now, and reversible, because it is a person reporting what happened in somebody else’s ledger.
  • Closing a collection immediately claimed “Banked in”. Closing, filing the form and the money landing are three events; the portal can observe none of them and was asserting the last. They are separate states now.
  • The declared-payments alert had no link on it — it said there was a queue and left you to find it.

Dropped: the next-reference-number panel, which is bookkeeping and is shown at the moment a number is allocated anyway. The reference-count tile became claims OSL have been sent and have not yet paid — the one number on that page that is money the club is owed rather than money it has.

The Income Deposit Form

The blank template arrived, so the figures became a generator. It is OSL’s own workbook with our numbers written into its blanks — their layout, their fonts, their borders, their GL account codes, their GST formulas — not a reconstruction that they may or may not accept. That is the whole point of a Class B document.

  • The breakdown goes by tier, not by member. That is what the form asks for — item, price, quantity — and forty-five lines reading “membership subscription, $70, 1” is a worse answer than one line saying forty-two. It also keeps the members’ names off a document that leaves the club.
  • Only money on the official route is counted. Anything the exco confirmed as held outside OSL was never banked into the DBS Max wallet, and including it would make the form disagree with the statement it gets checked against.
  • The template ships base64-encoded so it survives a standalone build. OSL’s document properties were stripped: they carried two staff members’ names and SUTD’s own sensitivity label.
  • The form has room for three breakdown rows. A club with four fee tiers is not an error, so the writer inserts rows — shifting merges, cell references and the total’s own range with them — rather than silently dropping the fourth and understating what was banked.
  • Where the club is not on 9% GST, it says so rather than overwriting OSL’s formula with our arithmetic.

It downloads as the spreadsheet — the working copy, the one OSL can reopen and correct — and prints as a PDF through the browser, the same way the coach pack does. The print view is a rendering of the workbook rather than a redesign of it, with the club’s mark in the corner. Both read one model, and a test reduces each to text and compares them, evaluating the workbook’s formulas rather than skipping the money as “a formula”, since the money is the part worth checking.

Whose rounding wins

OSL’s cells compute gross/109*100 and gross/109*9 and round each for display. The portal’s own splitGst divides and takes the remainder, which can put a stray cent somewhere else. The PDF follows the workbook, because a PDF that disagrees with the spreadsheet it accompanies is worse than one that rounds the way the recipient’s own template does.

The signatures, once real ones arrived

Six specimens went up, and they broke two assumptions.

  • The width floor was wrong. It asked for 200 pixels on the assumption that a signature is wide and short, and refused a real one that is 160×162 — which would have printed beautifully, because the box is far shorter than it is wide and a tall image is scaled down by its height. What matters is the resolution left after the fit, which is a fact about both dimensions and about the box. It is measured as effective dpi now, with a loose aspect bound to catch the 900×3 hairline that has plenty of dpi and is not handwriting. A test names all six.
  • One of the six is a photograph of ink on cream paper. Stamped onto a white form it is a coloured rectangle sitting over the signature line. The paper is lifted off now — alpha ramped between the paper and the ink rather than thresholded, because a threshold turns the edge of every stroke into a staircase and a signature is nothing but edges. The detector finds exactly that one and leaves the five clean captures alone.

The lifting happens in the browser, in front of the person whose signature it is, with both versions shown over a real signature rule so they can compare and refuse. Altering somebody’s signature silently on a server is not a thing to do.

The PayNow QR belongs to the collection

It was one nullable URL on organisation settings, which assumes the club has one QR forever. It does not — a new one is generated for each collection, so the term after the first would have shown members the previous term’s code and their money would have gone somewhere nobody was reconciling against this roster. It lives on the term now, uploaded on the collection screen, streamed by a route that re-checks membership on every request and is never cached. Where there is no QR yet, the member page says so rather than leaving a blank space they cannot interpret.

Still open

  • User acceptance. The automated suites cover the current workflows, but the active phase is a treasurer-led pass through each accepted scenario with disposable data. Defects found there are fixed before procurement, budgeting or contract administration expands the scope.
  • A spend-over-time chart. Now that the registers are imported there is enough dated history to draw one honestly — it was not worth adding while pre-portal spend was a single undated lump.
  • Four coach payments in the FY2026 register (June and July) carry no reference number at all. The numbers were never allocated, so the series has no gap to answer for, but the register rows cannot be tied back to a document.
  • IV24-097-0024 is missing from the FY2024 register entirely. Either a document was voided without being recorded as voided, or a row never got written down; nobody currently knows which, and OSL will ask.
  • Instructor photographs. The card is already built to take one — photoUrl is threaded through the component — but there is no column and no upload yet, so every plate is currently initials. The signature upload has now laid the private-bucket path it would follow.
  • Storage policies on storage.objects, added from the Supabase SQL editor. Signature access is authorised in the application today and there is nothing a migration here can do about the second layer.
  • Production authentication email. Supabase's built-in sender is for development and is too rate-limited for a club-wide signup. Configure a transactional SMTP provider in Supabase before launch; no application email SDK is needed. Email confirmation on sign-up is still on and has to be turned off from the Supabase dashboard — the portal holds no management credential for the project and the connector exposes no auth settings.
  • A real fee collection. The flow is built and nobody has run one through it. The first term it is used for should be one somebody is watching.
  • Reminders. The deadlines are computed and shown; nothing sends them yet. That is part of phase 06, after the existing portal passes user acceptance.
  • Procurement (phase 04). The schema models a Purchase Request and derives its form type from the amount; no screen reads it yet. When it arrives it is a third workflow, and the card and the strip on the home screen are already the shape it should take.
  • Insights, budget and full contract administration (phase 05). The planned Insights tab will answer how much should remain by March, where the club is over or under plan, and whether a proposed member-welfare spend fits after commitments. Monthly invoices and contract references already work; annual budgets, forecasts, scenario inputs, contract documents, total-hour allowances, remaining-hour alerts, renewals, evaluations and Purchase Award Forms do not.
  • Register sync writeback (phase 07). The registers import; nothing writes back to Drive.
  • A real treasurer has not yet accepted the portal. Verification so far is by typecheck, lint, production build, unit and integration tests, live-database browser journeys and rendered document comparisons. Phase 03 adds the human pass rather than treating automated coverage as acceptance.
